Pronouns refer to people or things without repeating their names. English uses forms such as I and he for subjects, and me and him for objects. Words such as my and your show possession. Before a noun, English grammar often calls these possessive determiners; here they are included under possessive pronouns.

  • “You [subject] watch [verb] me [direct object]”
  • “He [subject] finds [verb] my [possessive] house [direct object]”

This lesson groups Sindarin forms by their use: independent pronouns, subject suffixes, objects, datives, and possessives.

1.2.3a How to Read the Chart

First choose the row for the person and number, then choose the column for the pronoun's job.

  • Person: First person is the speaker, second person is the listener, and third person is someone or something being discussed.
  • Number: Singular refers to one; plural refers to more than one. Inclusive we includes the listener; exclusive we does not.
  • Register: Familiar and formal are different ways of addressing the listener. This chart follows the distinction I use throughout the course.
  • Role: A subject suffix tells you who performs the action; an object receives the action; a dative often means “to” or “for” someone; and a possessive tells you who something belongs to.
  • Spelling: Accents matter. The chart distinguishes nin “me” from nín “my”. A leading hyphen marks a suffix; it is not normally written separately from its verb.
About the evidence: * marks a reconstructed form or use, while † marks an archaic form. “No ending” means exactly that: no separate personal suffix is added; the chart is not missing a form. Because Tolkien's surviving pronoun paradigms are incomplete, this Neo-Sindarin chart necessarily combines attested and reconstructed forms. Even an unstarred form may only be attested in a different role or period. For example, de is attested as an archaic polite singular. See Eldamo's overview of pronoun evidence.

1.2.3.1 Independent and Object Pronouns

An independent pronoun is a separate word. It can stand as the subject of a statement without an expressed verb, or it can function as an object. You may see the labels nominative and accusative used for these forms, but the evidence does not consistently support a simple division between forms without final -n for subjects and forms with -n for objects. Both kinds can occur as objects. See independent pronouns for the evidence.

  • ci pron. “you”
  • le pron. “you”
  • de pron. “y'all”
  • ti pron. “they/them”
  • nin pron. “me”
  • men pron. “us”

Example sentences:

  • Ci nîn. “You [are] wet.”
  • Ni rochben. “I [am a] rider.”
  • Te chothron. “He/she/it [is a] captain.”
  • en·Orch vâd din. “The orc eats them.”
  • Hirin den. “I find him/her/it.”
What is a copula? It is the linking is or are in an English statement. Sindarin often leaves this link unspoken. Mutation in these statements is debated, especially with predicate nouns and adjectives. A common Neo-Sindarin approach leaves them unlenited, although I sometimes use lenition according to the copular-mutation preference explained later in the course. This is separate from the mutation of a finite verb after its subject. See Eldamo's discussion of the copula.

1.2.3.2 Subject Suffixes

A subject suffix tells you who performs the verb's action. When the suffix already identifies the subject, a separate pronoun is normally unnecessary. Choose the suffix for the subject, not for the object.

  • -n suf. “I”
  • -l suf. “you”
  • no ending 3rd singular “he/she/it”

For example:

  • Tirin en·orch. “I watch the orc.”
  • Medil in·aew. “You eat the birds.”
  • Sâf vegil. “He/she/it has [a] sword.”

The plural ending -r can agree with a stated plural subject or with an understood “they”. It does not necessarily add a separate pronoun to the sentence. See Verb Suffixes for the full set and how endings combine with different stems.

1.2.3.2a Dative Pronouns

A dative usually identifies the person or thing receiving something or benefiting from an action, often expressed with “to” or “for” in English. Dative pronouns are separate words, not subject endings.

My dative convention: I use mixed mutation in forming dative pronouns with an. Eldamo's recommended personal dative paradigm is mostly based on nasal mutation. Throughout this course, I therefore use the forms shown below, including angin, anden, andin, and inclusive amben. This difference from Eldamo's reconstruction is intentional.

The complete paradigm is partly reconstructed. The mutation convention identifies the forms used in this course; it does not make every form an attested Tolkien word. See Eldamo's dative-pronoun discussion for the evidence and its alternative reconstruction.

* marks a reconstructed course form. annin, ammen, and reflexive anim are attested. This course uses ammen for exclusive “us” and amben for inclusive “us”.

Dative experiencers: a dative can also identify the person experiencing an event in a construction supported for a particular verb. This extends its use beyond recipients and beneficiaries.

Parmandil suggested this analysis for the supplied lítha annin in a 12 April 2026 discussion of forgetting. His suggestion draws on older evidence and should not become a universal rule for every sense of the verb. See 1.2.2.3c Passive and Impersonal Constructions for the distinction.

1.2.3.2b Demonstratives

Demonstratives point to a person, place, or thing, or refer back to something already mentioned. Near forms correspond to “this/these”; remote forms correspond to “that/those”.

This is the demonstrative paradigm I use in this course. * marks reconstructed independent and dative forms. The object/adjective column gives base forms supported by Tolkien's examples and notes, sometimes only through their mutated forms. The evidence for exactly how each form was used is uneven, so the independent/object division here should be treated as a teaching arrangement rather than a firmly established paradigm.

As an adjective, a demonstrative normally follows its noun and undergoes soft mutation. The noun usually has a definite article and takes the mutation appropriate to that article. As a direct object, a demonstrative can instead be mutated because of its object position. Its visible initial alone does not identify its grammatical role.

Eldamo lists the same demonstrative datives, explaining them through nasal mutation and internal ss. This course keeps those forms while adopting mixed mutation for dative-pronoun formation. As elsewhere, final n alone does not reliably identify case.

A demonstrative can itself be described by a relative clause. The supplied san i sequence illustrates the two roles in “that which”. See 1.2.6.2d Demonstratives with Relative Clauses.

1.2.3.3 Possessive

Independent possessives normally follow the noun and undergo soft mutation themselves. A preceding article separately governs the noun's initial mutation. The following base forms are supported by Tolkien's examples, sometimes only through their mutated forms:

  • nín pron. “my”
  • mín pron. “our”
  • lín pron. “your”
  • tín pron. “his/her/its”
  • e·mbâr nín “my house”
  • e·'il nín “my star”
  • e·daur gín “your forest”
  • en·aran lín “your king”
  • en·aew dín “his/her bird”
  • e·vegill dín “his/her sword”

For a plural possessed noun, distinguish plural formation from article mutation. Form the noun's plural first; then apply the mutation required by its article. A possessive does not by itself impose nasal mutation on the noun. Its person and number refer to the possessor, not to how many things are possessed.

  • i·mbair nín “my houses”
  • in·erain gín “your kings”
  • in·aew dín “his/her birds”

In the last example, the definite article “in” tells you that the noun is plural.

My spelling style: I attach the article to its noun with a middle dot. At the start of a sentence, I capitalize the noun rather than the article. This is my presentation preference. The independent possessive remains a separate word.

Possessed nouns often have an article, with exceptions such as direct address and constructions involving proper names. Tolkien also used possessive suffixes. Their later paradigm is incomplete, so this lesson concentrates on independent forms. See possessive pronouns and suffixes.

Possessives within a larger phrase: In this course, a possessive placed after its noun can still remain closely attached to that noun as part of the subject phrase, even though it is written as a separate word. I treat this as an enclitic use, so it does not interrupt subject-to-verb mutation. A truly independent word placed between the completed subject phrase and the verb does interrupt that relationship.

A possessive following a coordinated group of nouns can also refer to the whole group. If it makes every member possessive and definite, each noun must satisfy the article requirements for its own construction. These are the scope conventions used in this course.

1.2.3.3a Reflexive Possession

The King's Letter contains the possessive în in this sentence: ar e aníra ennas suilannad mhellyn în phain “and he desires to greet there all his friends.” ✧ AotM/62; SD/129

Stepanov's proposal: In a Discord discussion, Venceslao Stepanov connects this passage with the distinction he identifies in PE23 between third-person singular subject e and oblique te. In his reading of the King's Letter, e and în refer to Aragorn, while dîn refers to Samwise in the passage about Samwise's wife and children.

Stepanov suggests that în may depend specifically on subject e, and questions whether it can still be used when de replaces that subject. The de in this proposal is being discussed in a third-person role; it should not be confused with the second-person use listed in the chart.

What this means for the lesson: His proposal questions whether the use illustrated by the King's Letter can be extended to other subjects. A general reflexive use of ín remains uncertain, and the proposed dependence on e is a hypothesis rather than a confirmed restriction.

Source: Venceslao Stepanov, Discord discussion of PE23. His separate comment describing reflexive ín as problematic refers back to this concern. For the broader uncertainty, see Eldamo's discussion of reflexive possessives.

The quoted King's Letter passage uses în; my earlier examples below retain the spelling ín.

A reflexive pronoun refers back to the subject. Late evidence treats im as a general reflexive, rather than exclusively first person. That does not settle the possessive în. See independent and reflexive pronouns.

Community discussions: athrabeth and eldamo on Discord.

Earlier examples of the reflexive interpretation

These are my earlier examples, retained with their original spelling. They show how I was using ín before those discussions. Their extension of reflexive possession to different subjects remains uncertain in light of the proposal above.

Elrond loves his dog.

Who does his refer to? It could be that Elrond loves his own dog, or his friend's dog (Celebrimbor's dog).

  • Elrond vêl e·chû ín. (Elrond loves his (own) dog.)
  • Elrond vêl e·chû dín. (Elrond loves his (Celebrimbor's) dog.)

Additional examples:

  • Arwen dâg e·'annel ín. (Arwen fixes her (own) harp.)
  • Arwen dâg e·'annel dín. (Arwen fixes her harp (someone else's harp.))

Plural subject examples:

  • Melir e·chû ín. (They love their own dog.)
  • Melir e·chû dín. (They love their dog (it could be their friend's dog.))

With multiple subjects:

  • Te ah e·hell ín dorthar v’ Imladris. (He and his daughter live in Rivendell.)
  • Te ah e·vellon dín menir n’e·'obel. (He and his friend go to the village.)
  • Arwen ah i·chenair ín dorthar v’ Imladris. (Arwen and her brothers live in Rivendell.)

These words refer to unspecified people or things, express quantity, or describe a place. They belong together as a useful vocabulary group, although they do not all have the same grammatical function.

About ae: My source comparison is Quenya ai², listed as “supposing, suppose, maybe; *any”. Eldamo identifies the sense “any” as a scholarly suggestion. That comparison supports a proposed use; it does not attest Sindarin ae with this meaning. The separate Neo-Sindarin ae entry concerns “if”. The table records my intended “any” sense explicitly.

About ln and ll: The alternatives ilnad and illad reflect different treatments of ln in compounds. Eldamo prefers retaining ln in newer formations; Elaran's illad and allad apply the historical change to ll. Both approaches are discussed in the linked entries. I keep the forms used in my word list here for consistency.

Pay attention to what the word is doing: ae and ail belong with determiners and quantifiers in these uses. The words referring to people and things can stand where a noun would stand. ilhad and alhad tell you where something happens, so I treat them here as place adverbs. A dictionary may group some of these words under pronouns, but their function in the sentence still matters.

1.2.3.3c My Usage Conventions

The following conventions explain some of the choices you will see in my examples. Other Neo-Sindarin writers may follow different analyses or spelling conventions.

Dative pronouns and mixed mutation

I use mixed mutation for dative-pronoun formation with an. Follow the course dative table and demonstrative table when reading my examples. Eldamo instead generally reconstructs these forms with nasal mutation.

After ail, il, and er

I use liquid mutation after ail “each/every”, il “all”, and er “one, sole, only”. The treatment of ail follows the same course convention because it ends in l. The result depends on the following word's original consonant and historical initial. Use the course mutation chart for the relevant form.

Eldamo treats liquid mutation as optional for Neo-Sindarin, because the surviving evidence is limited and mixed. In this course, however, I use it consistently in these environments. This word-boundary rule is separate from the historical formation of compounds such as ilphen.

Negative words and negative concord

Words such as alphen, úben, allad, and alhad already express a negative meaning. In this course, I allow a negative indefinite or negative adverb to carry the negative meaning of the whole clause without automatically adding another negative particle or negative verb. The surviving evidence does not establish one required pattern for every situation. This follows the PE23/99 principle cited in my reference notes that negation was normally combined with the pronoun or a derived adverb.

Two negative expressions can also work together to express one negative meaning. This is called negative concord. So seeing two negative expressions together does not automatically mean something has gone wrong. For the wider historical background, see Sindarin negation.

Place words and mutation

In this course, I also apply mutation to adverbs in environments such as after a verb. This is broader than the practice followed in some other Neo-Sindarin descriptions. This includes words functioning as place adverbs. The table gives their base forms; any visible change depends on the word's initial sound and the surrounding construction.

Articles and historical spelling

I use the late singular articles e/en and plural i/in, together with the mutation system followed throughout this course. Where applicable, I retain historical mb-, nd-, and ñg- spellings to show a word's original initial. spellings to show a word's original initial. These conventions account for some of the spellings you may notice in examples with possessives. See Definite Articles and the note on possessive scope.

1.2.3.3d Possessives and Adjectives

A possessive following a descriptive adjective can still be lenited. Tolkien's prayer uses the order noun + adjective + possessive for “our daily bread”, with the possessive mutated. See Eldamo's possessive-pronoun discussion and its VT44/21 reference. This is why the ordinary adjective-list pattern should not automatically be extended to possessives.

Strack tentatively connects phain in the King's Letter to the preceding possessive. This does not establish a general nasal-mutation rule after possessives. See his discussion of adjectives and possessive pronouns.

That proposal concerns the following adjective's initial. The separate question of whose possession în can express remains in Reflexive Possession. Neither question changes my convention on possessives within the subject phrase.

1.2.3.4 Section Summary

Here is a simplified chart of the personal pronouns discussed in this chapter.

Three roles at a glance
ObjectSubject SuffixPossessive
nin “me”-n “I”nín “my”
*len “you”-l “you”lín “your”
*ten “him, her”no ending “he, she”tín “his, her”

* retains the reconstruction marker used in the main chart.

  • Read the role in context. Final n alone does not identify an object.
  • Subject endings belong to the verb. Third singular has no separate personal ending; -r is the plural ending.
  • Distinguish the dative from the direct object. A dative identifies a recipient or beneficiary. This course uses mixed mutation for dative-pronoun formation.
  • Recognize lexical dative uses. Certain constructions can express an experiencer with a dative; the proposed forgetting example belongs to that discussion.
  • Read demonstratives by their role. An independent pronoun, a direct object, and an adjective modifying a noun follow different constructions. The complete demonstrative paradigm is partly reconstructed.
  • Keep demonstratives and relatives distinct. In the supplied han i construction, the demonstrative supplies the referent and the relative marker introduces its description.
  • Keep possessors and possessions distinct. The possessive follows the noun and is normally lenited. The article governs the noun's mutation.
  • Keep the evidence markers. Reconstructed forms and reflexive possession require more caution than well-attested uses.
  • Distinguish the related word groups. Indefinite pronouns, quantifiers, and place adverbs have different jobs, even when they appear in one vocabulary table.
  • Remember the course conventions. These include liquid mutation after ail/il/er, negative words that can carry the clause's negation, and possessives that remain within the subject phrase.

This lesson covers personal pronouns, demonstratives, and selected indefinite, quantity, and place words. For later study, see interrogative pronouns.
