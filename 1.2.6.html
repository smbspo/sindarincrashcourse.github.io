Conjunctions connect words, phrases, or clauses. Relative clauses describe a noun, while other subordinate clauses can supply a time, reason, purpose, or the content of a statement. Identifying the relationship makes a longer sentence easier to read.

1.2.6a Reading the Examples

What is being connected?
Construction English cue Role
Coordination and, or, but Connects elements that have the same kind of grammatical role, such as two nouns or two complete clauses.
Relative clause who, which, that Describes or adds information about an antecedent, the noun or noun phrase the relative clause refers to.
Time clause while, when Relates the timing of one event to another.
Conditional clause if States a condition on which a consequence depends.
Reason or purpose because; in order to Gives a reason or an intended aim. These are different relationships.

Conjunctions and clause links used in this lesson:

  • ah conj. “and”
  • ach² conj. “but”
  • egor conj. “or”
  • lo conj. “while”
  • io conj. “when”
  • pi adv. “if” (conditional link)

Related pronouns: these have different jobs from the conjunctions above.

  • pen² pron. “one, somebody”
  • i² pron. “who, that”
  • man pron. “*what, who (Interrogative)”

The small superscript numbers distinguish dictionary entries with the same spelling. In particular, pronoun pen² differs from the preposition in the previous chapter, and relative i² differs in function from an article.

lo is a Neo-Sindarin formation credited to Elaran, and io is credited to Röandil. Both are already listed by Eldamo, so I am not introducing them as vocabulary unique to this course. For the other conjunctions, see a(h), ach, and egor.

1.2.6.1 Conjunctions

Notice what the conjunction joins in each example. The first joins two subjects; the second connects two clauses, each with its own subject and verb.

  • en-Edhel ah en·Adan lerir vi e·mâr. “The elf and the man sleep in the house.”
  • en·Adan vên n’ e·garas ach en·edhel vên n’ e·daur. “The man is going to the city, but the elf is going to the forest.”
a or ah: I use a before consonants and ah before vowels, and I apply sibilant mutation after them. Eldamo currently recommends this pattern as well, following the PE23 evidence that conjunctions induce mutation. See Eldamo's a(h) discussion.

The core sibilant changes discussed here are:

  • p, t, c → ph, th, ch.
  • h → ch.
  • l, r → lh, rh, with possible historical variation.

The fuller pattern has limited evidence, especially outside its core consonant changes. Eldamo's sibilant-mutation discussion distinguishes the recorded description from reconstructed details. The pattern for a(h) is not automatically the pattern for every conjunction.

1.2.6.1a Coordination and Mutation

In my usage, a coordinated phrase is one grammatical unit. If a finite verb is immediately followed by a coordinated direct object, its object mutation applies at the beginning of that whole phrase. Later members do not each receive another mutation from the earlier verb.

Two sources of mutation in a coordinated object
Relationship Where it acts Course convention
Finite verb and its object phrase The beginning of the following coordinated object Apply the verb's object mutation once to the phrase, rather than independently to every noun inside it.
Conjunction or article and the following word A boundary inside the phrase A later member can still undergo the mutation required by its own conjunction, article, or other local relationship.

Strack's Nolmar draft likewise mutates only the first object in a list immediately after its verb. His broader treatment of displaced objects still differs from the adjacency convention I use here. Compare Eldamo's direct-object discussion.

For several modifiers describing one noun, see 1.2.4.1a Lists of Adjectives.

A following possessive can sometimes refer to an entire coordinated noun phrase. When the intended meaning makes all its members definite, each member still needs the appropriate definiteness marking. Do not assume that the article on one noun automatically makes every other noun in the coordinated phrase definite.

Simple noun lists can also occur without a final ah/ar. This is supported by the late King's Letter discussion and is also noted in Eldamo's conjunction entry. It is an option for simple lists, not a rule for removing conjunctions from any pair of clauses.

Consonantal i and the course's sibilant analysis

An initial i before another vowel can represent consonantal /j/. In this course, I retain my sibilant treatment of that historical initial after a(h) and o(h). This goes beyond the better-established core sibilant changes listed above, so I treat it as an additional course proposal. A word beginning with consonantal i should not be treated as vowel-initial simply because of its spelling.

1.2.6.1b Conditional Clauses

A conditional clause states what must hold for a consequence to follow. In the English example “If the road is clear, we will leave”, “if the road is clear” gives the condition and “we will leave” gives the consequence. The condition is not asserted to be true.

I use pi as a conditional link. Eldamo lists pi as a Neo-Sindarin formation credited to Fiona Jallings, so the vocabulary choice is shared with other Neo-Sindarin usage.

Different clause relationships: “if” sets a condition; temporal “when” places an event in time; “until” sets a time boundary. English “then” can point to a consequence or to a time. Identify its job before deciding how to express the relationship.

My vocabulary distinction: conditional pi and the ae used for “any” in my indefinite chart have different jobs.

The March 2026 Discord discussion includes competing proposals for mutation after pi and for the time-linking forms naden and ten. It does not establish a single agreed pattern. The sibilant pattern described for a(h) should not automatically be assigned to every conditional link.

1.2.6.2 Subordinate Clauses

A subordinate clause depends on another clause. A relative clause is one kind: it describes an antecedent. In “I watch the man who runs”, “who runs” refers to “man”. The supplied Sindarin example uses i: Tirin en·adan i nôr.

Relative i can refer to people or things. English “who”, “which”, and “that” do not each require a separate Sindarin relative form. Compare the English descriptions “I am watching the man that is running” and “he finds a tower that has a roof”. The noun being described is the antecedent in each case.

  • Hîr vinas i hâf rond. “He/She finds a tower that has a roof”, where hîr = “finds-he”.
  • Riston en·aew i aran mâd. “I cut the bird that a king eats”, where risto-n = “(cutting)-I”.

Indeclinable simply means that the particle does not have an ordinary set of case forms. That is a separate question from the article-like restrictive construction I use below. Neither idea gives us a complete Sindarin paradigm corresponding to English forms such as “to whom” or “whose”.

1.2.6.2a Restrictive and Non-restrictive Relatives

A restrictive relative helps identify which person or thing is meant. A non-restrictive relative adds information about a referent already identified. English commas often help show the latter, but the intended meaning and context come first.

In this course, I follow the Luinyelle/Lanto analysis described below. This table shows the interpretation I have adopted from the available evidence; it should not be read as a fully settled Tolkienian paradigm.

Relative mutation in this course
Relative construction Antecedent Mutation caused by the relative marker
Restrictive iSingularSoft (vocalic), following the article-like analysis.
Restrictive iPluralNasal, following the article-like analysis.
Non-restrictive iSingular or pluralNone from the relative marker.
Restrictive aiSingular or pluralSoft; treated here as emphatic “which/who”, without separately marking plurality.
Non-restrictive aiSingular or pluralNone from the relative marker.

None in the table means that the relative marker contributes no mutation. Ordinary grammatical relationships inside the clause can still cause their own mutations. Article-like behavior also does not mean replacing every relative i with the course's ordinary singular article e/en.

What controls the number? Use the antecedent's number, not the number of whichever noun happens to follow i. In the supplied i erain medir example, the relative describes singular en·aew. The plural erain inside that clause does not make its antecedent plural.

The tower example above is being read restrictively: “which has a roof” helps identify the tower. Under that reading, singular soft mutation explains hâf beside the reference form sâf.

For the source forms and examples, see Eldamo's entries for relative i and relative ai. Those entries do not establish the entire table above. In particular, my treatment of ai does not require interpreting it as an exclusively plural form.

Further examples from the supplied lesson:

  • Tirin edain i nerir. “I watch men who run.”, alternatively with a participle, Tirin edain norel.
  • Hîr vinais i rynd sevir. “He finds towers that have roofs.”
  • Riston in·Aew i aran mâd. “I cut the birds that a king eats.”
  • Riston en·Aew i erain medir. “I cut the bird that kings eat”
Earlier discussion of word order

An earlier discussion read Hîr vinais i rynd sevir. as putting more emphasis on what the towers have. Another possible order is Hîr vinais i sevir rynd.. Under that reading, restrictive i still follows the plural nasal-mutation pattern because its antecedent is plural. The initial s of sevir remains unchanged.

Identify the subject and object within the relative clause, then relate the clause to its antecedent. A noun placed before a verb is not necessarily its subject when an object has been moved for emphasis.

1.2.6.2b Relative ias and Clause Attachment

I use ias for relative “where” and apply soft mutation to the following word. This is a separate course convention from the restrictive/non-restrictive treatment of i/ai.

Eldamo's ias entry marks the modern formation as discouraged and lists it as an adverb in the question category. I use ias specifically as a relative “where”; Eldamo's dictionary entry does not establish the soft-mutation convention I use with it.

When a relative follows a genitive phrase or a prepositional phrase, its antecedent is not automatically the nearest noun. Use number, meaning, and the surrounding context to determine which noun or phrase it describes. If two materially different readings remain plausible, the sentence needs more context.

1.2.6.2c Reason and Purpose

A connection can be expressed by a phrase rather than a single conjunction. These are the forms and literal glosses used in my course:

Introducing a reason or referring back to one
Course phrase Supplied literal gloss Role
añ gaeth ifor reason thatIntroduces the clause giving the reason.
eni gaeth hanfor that reasonRefers to a reason identified in context.

These phrases have different structures, so do not treat them as interchangeable just because English can express both ideas with a short linking phrase. My first form follows the mixed-mutation convention I use with an; Eldamo's corresponding reason expression uses nasal mutation.

A purpose states an intended aim. The proposed use of an with a verbal noun belongs to that discussion. See 1.2.5.1a Purpose Clauses with an for Ellanto's examples and their distinction between purpose and a complement of “wanted”.

1.2.6.2d Demonstratives with Relative Clauses

A demonstrative can supply an antecedent for a relative clause. English “that which” makes the two jobs visible: “that” supplies a referent, and “which” introduces the clause describing it.

The two roles in the supplied han i sequence
Supplied form Role in the discussion What to identify
sanThe demonstrative serving as the object in the larger clause.What the larger clause refers to.
iThe marker introducing the relative clause.The clause that describes that referent.

Ellanto explicitly retained both san and i in this construction during the March 2026 discussion. This is a different structure from a relative clause directly describing an already expressed noun. The presence of “that” in an English sentence is not enough to decide which structure it uses.

The supplied sequence illustrates the roles, not an invariant form for every relative construction. Apply the course's restrictive and non-restrictive analysis to the intended antecedent and clause. See also Demonstratives.

1.2.6.3 Distribution of Meaning Among Words

A word's English gloss gives a starting meaning, not every possible grammatical use of that English word. English “that” can point something out, introduce a relative clause, or introduce the content of a statement. Those functions should be distinguished before choosing a Sindarin form.

Likewise, English “while” can link events in time or contrast two statements. The temporal use of lo in this vocabulary list should not automatically be extended to every contrastive use of English “while”. Conjunctional io also has a different job from asking a question about time.

In a relative clause, keep two questions separate: which noun is being described, and what grammatical role that noun has inside the clause. The noun may be the subject or object inside the relative clause. Choosing a relative construction does not remove ordinary number agreement or the other grammatical relationships inside that clause.

Vocabulary distinctions raised in the older corrections
English wording Distinction to preserve Why it matters
conceiveAn idea or a childThe same English verb covers different events.
earthMaterial or the WorldA shared gloss does not make the senses interchangeable.
crafts and craftspeopleAn activity or those who perform itThe noun must identify the intended thing or participants.
own and propertyA modifier or a nounA word meaning “property” does not automatically have the grammar of English “own”.
evil or secretA description or a noun naming an idea or thingAn adjective gloss alone does not establish the required noun use.
payGiving money or atoningEnglish can extend a verb figuratively; the corresponding Sindarin sense needs support.

Some adjectives can be used substantivally, but that use needs its own support. Identify the intended grammatical role instead of assuming that every English noun and adjective with the same spelling share one Sindarin form. For verbs, see Verb Meanings and Objects.

Reading a dictionary entry: note its part of speech, the particular sense, and the language stage. A historical root is a source for related words; it is not automatically a Sindarin word ready to use. An older Noldorin or Gnomish entry needs to be considered alongside the form adopted for this course.

Neo-Sindarin labels identify modern formations or adaptations. Many are useful course vocabulary. Distinguish an adopted choice from a securely attested form, and keep a speaker's tentative suggestion identified as tentative.

Some choices below agree with other Neo-Sindarin resources, while others are interpretations I use specifically in this course.

1.2.6.4 Section Summary

  • Identify what is joined: conjunctions can connect words, phrases, or complete clauses.
  • Distinguish a relative clause: it describes an antecedent, the noun or noun phrase it refers to.
  • Use the course's relative analysis: restrictive i takes soft or nasal mutation according to the antecedent's number; restrictive ai takes soft mutation. Non-restrictive i/ai contribute no mutation themselves.
  • Keep mutation boundaries separate: mutation of a coordinated object phrase and mutation caused by a conjunction inside it are different relationships.
  • Read linking phrases by their role: a reason, a reference back to a reason, and an intended purpose are different connections.
  • Separate condition and consequence: conditional pi has a different function from time links and from the ae used for “any” in this course.
  • Recognize both roles in han i: a demonstrative can supply the referent that a relative clause describes.
  • Compare meanings in context: one English gloss does not give a Sindarin word every possible use of its English counterpart.
