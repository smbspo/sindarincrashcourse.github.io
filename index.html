This course explores Sindarin, an Elvish language created by J.R.R. Tolkien, prominently featured in his Middle-earth saga. Sindarin, often termed “Common Elven,” contrasts with the “High Elven” tongue Quenya. Originating with the Elves of Beleriand, Sindarin evolved in Middle-earth, distinct from the Quenya spoken across the Sea. Its rich history includes adoption by the Dúnedain and influence on placenames like Minas Tirith. This course requires no prior knowledge of Elvish languages, but learning its grammar takes practice.

The lessons introduce grammar, sound mutations, and the patterns behind Tolkien’s linguistic design. Because the surviving material is incomplete, learning Sindarin also means understanding where the evidence ends and interpretation begins.

If you want a quick overview of what Sindarin is like, check out the basic grammar entry in the Eldamo lexicon. Also check out Celephinnel's site.

Please note that there are many outdated and inaccurate resources out there. For those considering tattoos, remember: the language is incomplete and new discoveries can change things. Most recommend writing English in Tengwar script instead. If you want a Sindarin name, learn the basics first, or ask on one of the linked sites.

1.1.1 History of Sindarin

Sindarin, the language of the Grey Elves of Middle-earth, has its roots in the Common Quenderin language, first spoken by the Elves at Cuiviénen (S. Nen Echui). It evolved from the dialect of the Teleri, the third tribe of Elves. The language's development reflects the Elves' complex history, including the division of the Teleri and the emergence of the Sindar in Beleriand.

During the ancient Telerin period, the language began diverging, especially when a faction led by Elwë (Thingol) remained in Middle-earth. This group, the Sindar, saw their language evolve independently. Despite later influence from Noldorin exiles and wars with Morgoth, Sindarin remained the dominant Elvish tongue in western Middle-earth.

Sindarin’s linguistic journey is marked by several periods: Common Quenderin, Common Eldarin, Ancient Telerin, and Old Sindarin. The Late Sindarin period, spanning the Second and Third Ages, is notable for stability, possibly influenced by the Elven Rings.

Tolkien’s notes highlight Sindarin’s gradual dominance and rich evolution. This course offers a guided tour of its history and significance in Tolkien’s world.

1.1.1a Pronunciation and Spelling

The course uses Roman letters. Acute accents and circumflexes mark vowel length; keep them when copying a word. Stress is governed by the syllable pattern, so an accent is not simply a stress mark.

A few sounds to learn before the first lesson
SpellingSound
a, e, i, o, uSteady vowels: roughly ah, eh, ee, oh, oo.
yA vowel like French u or German ü: say ee with rounded lips.
c, gHard sounds, as in English cat and get.
chThe sound in Scottish loch, represented by IPA [x].
th, dhRespectively the consonant sounds in thin and this.
Final f; phFinal f sounds like v. The spelling ph represents an f sound.
Initial i before a vowelThe consonant sound at the beginning of yes, IPA [j].
ñ / ngñ represents [ŋ], the final sound in sing. The spelling ng can be ambiguous; check its historical form and context.

For vowel lengths, diphthongs, and further spelling details, see Eldamo’s pronunciation and transcription guide, which discusses Tolkien’s Appendix E.

Finding the stressed syllable

In a two-syllable word, stress the first syllable. With three or more syllables, stress the second-to-last if it has a long vowel, a diphthong, or a vowel followed by two or more consonants; otherwise stress the third-to-last. Count sounds: th, dh, and ch each represent one consonant. Medial m and ph need special attention because of their historical length.

1.1.2 Mutations of Sindarin

Vowel changes help form many noun plurals. In English, “man” becomes “men”; Sindarin also uses vowel changes in plural formation. For example, adan (man) becomes edain (men). Some plurals are unchanged, irregular, or affected by the word’s history. Class plurals have their own formations. The noun lesson explains these patterns in more detail.

Initial-consonant mutation is a separate process: a word’s beginning changes because of its grammatical environment. For instance, adjectives after nouns may change their initial consonant: “the great man” is i adan veleg; “the great men” is in edain velig.

Vocalic mutation is another name for soft consonant mutation; it is distinct from the vowel changes used in forming plurals.

Reading the chart

Historical initials: [m]b-, [n]d-, [n]g-
The brackets identify an older nasal-plus-stop beginning. Even when a dictionary form begins with b-, d-, or g-, its mutation may follow this historical series.
Parentheses and restored spellings
Forms such as m(b)-, n(d)-, and ñ(g)- show reduced or restored spellings. Retaining mb-, nd-, and ñg- makes the historical initial visible. These are spelling alternatives within a mutation class.
The apostrophe ’
In this chart it marks the loss of an initial consonant. It does not tell you to add another consonant sound.
The dagger † and later usage
The thl-/thr- outcomes shown for lh-/rh- are archaic. Later Sindarin generally retains lh-/rh- without mutation. The change m- to v- may also be optional or absent in later usage, particularly with adjectives.
